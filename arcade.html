Chapter 01 · 1962—1985

The arcade went home.

For two decades, games moved from a machine in a public space to a box under the television. The controller came with it.

Years
1962—1985
Exhibits
04
Focus
Public play → the living room

1962

Spacewar!

Before home consoles, there was a laboratory. Spacewar! turned a PDP-1 into a two-player duel: two ships, a star, and a gravity well. It was not sold in shops, but it proved that a computer could be a place for play.

Why it mattered

It made the screen a shared space. Two people could sit at one machine, compete, and understand the rules without a manual.

1972

Pong

Two paddles, one ball, and a score. Pong took a simple idea and made it public: a machine in a bar, a line of people waiting, and a game anyone could understand in seconds.

Why it mattered

It turned play into a social routine. The rules were visible on the screen, and the competition did not need a manual.

1977

Atari 2600

A console with swappable cartridges brought arcade games home. The box under the television was no longer limited to the games built into it; the library could grow.

Why it mattered

The platform became a library. Games could be bought, collected, and traded, and the living room became a place for play.

1983 / 1985

Famicom / NES

Nintendo's family computer combined a cheap console, a controller with a cross-shaped pad, and games designed for the home. In North America, the NES helped rebuild the market after the crash of 1983.

Why it mattered

The console became a family object. The controller, the television, and the living room were now part of the same designed experience.
